Cryptographic attacks target weaknesses in encryption algorithms, implementations, or protocols rather than always trying to
crack the underlying math directly. Some of the most common types include:

- **Brute-Force Attacks:** Systematically trying every possible combination of keys or passwords until the correct one is
  found. This is heavily dependent on key length and processing power.
- **Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) Attacks:** An attacker secretly intercepts and relays communications between two parties who
  believe they are directly communicating with each other, allowing the attacker to eavesdrop or modify data.
- **Dictionary and Rainbow Table Attacks:** Instead of guessing randomly, attackers use precomputed tables of hashed
  passwords (rainbow tables) or lists of common words and phrases (dictionaries) to quickly match and crack hashed
  credentials.
- **Replay Attacks:** A form of network attack where valid data transmission is maliciously or fraudulently repeated or
  delayed. This often happens when an attacker intercepts a legitimate authentication session token and reuses it to gain
  unauthorized access.
- **Side-Channel Attacks:** These don't attack the cryptographic algorithm itself, but rather analyze physical
  implementations, such as measuring the precise time it takes a device to process data, its power consumption, or
  electromagnetic leaks to infer secret keys.
- **Chosen-Plaintext and Chosen-Ciphertext Attacks:** Scenarios where an attacker can influence the encryption or decryption
  processes by supplying specific inputs (plaintexts or ciphertexts) and analyzing the outputs to look for structural
  patterns or vulnerabilities in the cipher.
- **Hash Collisions (Birthday Attacks):** Exploiting mathematical flaws in hash functions where two different inputs produce
  the exact same hash output, potentially allowing attackers to forge digital signatures or certificates.
