Classical cryptography encompasses historical ciphers and encryption methods used before the advent of computers and digital
data processing, relying primarily on hand-written techniques, mechanical devices, or simple letter manipulation.

### Substitution Ciphers

Substitution ciphers replace individual letters or groups of letters with other letters, numbers, or symbols.

- **Caesar Cipher:** A monoalphabetic substitution cipher where each letter in the plaintext is shifted a fixed number of
  positions down the alphabet (for example, a shift of 3 turns "A" into "D").
- **Atbash Cipher:** A simple substitution cipher that reverses the alphabet, mapping the first letter to the last, the
  second to the second-to-last, and so on (so "A" becomes "Z").
- **Vigenère Cipher:** A polyalphabetic substitution cipher that uses a repeating keyword and a 26x26 grid (tabula recta) to
  apply varying shift amounts to each letter, making it much more resistant to traditional frequency analysis than
  single-alphabet ciphers.

### Transposition Ciphers

Unlike substitution ciphers that change the identity of letters, transposition ciphers keep the original letters intact but
scramble or rearrange their order.

- **Rail Fence Cipher:** The plaintext is written diagonally down a series of rows (like the rails of a fence), and then read
  off horizontally to produce the ciphertext.
- **The Scytale:** Used by ancient Spartans, a strip of leather or parchment was wrapped around a cylinder of a specific
  diameter, and the message was written lengthwise. When unwrapped, the letters appeared scrambled unless wrapped around an
  identical cylinder.

### Mechanical Cryptosystems

As technology progressed into the 20th century, classical cryptography evolved from paper-and-pencil methods into complex
electromechanical machines.

- **Enigma Machine:** A rotor-based cipher machine used extensively by Nazi Germany during World War II. It used a series of
  rotating wheels (rotors) and a plugboard to route electrical signals through a constantly changing, polyalphabetic
  substitution circuit with every keystroke.

### Limitations of Classical Cryptography

Virtually all classical cryptographic systems have been broken. Because they were designed for manual or mechanical
operation, they preserve statistical patterns found in natural human languages—such as the frequency of vowels like "E" or
"A"—allowing codebreakers to crack them using frequency analysis, pattern recognition, or basic computer brute-force attacks.
