A polyalphabetic cipher is a substitution cipher that uses multiple substitution alphabets to encrypt a message, making it
vastly more resistant to traditional frequency analysis than monoalphabetic ciphers like the Caesar cipher.

### How It Works

Instead of applying a single fixed rule or shift to every letter in a message, a polyalphabetic cipher changes the
substitution alphabet regularly throughout the encryption process, typically controlled by a repeating keyword or a
pre-shared sequence.

Because of this shifting approach, the same letter in a plaintext message can be encrypted as completely different ciphertext
characters depending on its position. For example, if the letter "E" appears three times in a sentence, a polyalphabetic
cipher might encrypt the first "E" as "X", the second as "Q", and the third as "M". This flattens out the natural frequency
distribution of human languages, preventing codebreakers from easily guessing letters based on how often they appear.

### Classic Examples

- **Vigenère Cipher:** A classic paper-and-pencil cipher that uses a keyword and a 26x26 grid (the tabula recta) to
  cyclically shift the alphabet for each consecutive letter of the plaintext.
- **Enigma Machine:** An advanced electromechanical rotor machine used during World War II that acted as a complex, automated
  polyalphabetic cipher, changing its internal wiring paths with every single keystroke.

### Security Status

While polyalphabetic ciphers were considered unbreakable for centuries, they are completely insecure by modern standards.
Mathematical techniques developed in the 19th century—such as the Kasiski examination and the index of coincidence—can
determine keyword lengths and break these ciphers within seconds using basic computing power.
