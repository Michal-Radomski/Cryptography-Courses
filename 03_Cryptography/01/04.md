The Enigma machine was an electromechanical rotor-based cipher device used extensively by the German military during World
War II to securely transmit operational messages. Resembling a modified typewriter, it scrambled text through a complex
combination of internal wiring and electrical pathways.

### How the Enigma Machine Worked

- **The Keyboard and Lampboard:** When an operator pressed a key on the keyboard, an electrical circuit was completed.
  Instead of printing, a corresponding letter lit up on a board of lightbulbs (the lampboard), showing the encrypted
  substitution.
- **The Rotors (Wheels):** The electricity flowed through a series of rotating disks called rotors (usually 3 to 5 were
  available, with 3 chosen for a given session). Each rotor had internal wiring that mapped input letters to completely
  different output letters.
- **The Stepping Mechanism:** Every time a key was pressed, the right-hand rotor stepped forward by one position (much like
  an odometer), and occasionally turned adjacent rotors. Because the rotors shifted with every single keystroke, the exact
  same letter typed twice in a row would map to completely different ciphertext letters. This created a dynamic
  polyalphabetic substitution cipher.
- **The Reflector:** After passing through the rotors, the current hit a reflector at the end of the line, which bounced the
  signal back through the rotors a second time on a different path before lighting up the bulb.
- **The Plugboard (Steckerbrett):** Located on the front of the machine, the plugboard allowed operators to use cables to
  swap pairs of letters before and after the signal passed through the rotors (e.g., connecting A to G). This added an
  exponential layer of complexity.

The vast number of possible daily settings—stemming from rotor choices, starting positions, and plugboard connections—gave
rise to trillions of possible configurations, leading the German military to believe the machine was unbreakable.

### How It Was Broken

Breaking the Enigma was a monumental collaborative effort involving Polish and British mathematicians, most notably Marian
Rejewski and Alan Turing. Because the German military changed the machine's global settings every single day, brute-forcing
the code by hand was impossible. Instead, codebreakers exploited human habits, structural flaws, and mathematical logic:

- **The Polish Breakthrough (1932):** Led by mathematician Marian Rejewski, the Polish Cipher Bureau mathematically deduced
  the internal wiring of the secret military rotors by exploiting flaws in how German operators transmitted message keys.
  They built early mechanical devices called _bomba_ to automate the search for settings.
- **Cribs (Known Plaintext):** Codebreakers looked for "cribs"—predictable phrases they expected to find in a message, such
  as weather reports ("WETTER"), administrative check-ins, or repetitive sign-offs like "Heil Hitler." If a cryptanalyst
  could guess where a specific word sat inside the ciphertext, they could test the logical consistency of the machine's
  loops.
- **The British Bombe (Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman):** When the Germans drastically increased security at the outbreak of
  war by changing cipher procedures and adding more rotor options, the Polish methods stalled and were handed over to British
  intelligence at Bletchley Park. Alan Turing, alongside Gordon Welchman, designed the **Bombe**—a massive electromechanical
  machine. The Bombe simulated the action of dozens of Enigma machines simultaneously, rapidly cycling through rotor
  combinations to test "cribs" and deduce the correct daily settings in a matter of minutes rather than weeks.
- **Traffic Analysis and Captured Material:** Allied codebreakers were further aided by captured Enigma codebooks, operating
  manuals, and careless operator behaviors (such as reusing the same key settings or sending identical messages twice).

By reading the intercepted Enigma traffic (intelligence code-named **Ultra**), the Allies gained vital insight into Axis
military movements, significantly shortening the war.
