A collision attack on MD5 is a cryptographic attack that finds two entirely different input messages (let's call them Message
A and Message B) that produce the exact same 128-bit MD5 hash output.

### How It Works

MD5 processes data in 512-bit blocks using a structure called the Merkle–Damgård construction, passing data through a series
of compression function steps.

Because MD5 is mathematically flawed, researchers discovered that they could systematically introduce controlled differences
into specific bits of two different blocks. By carefully crafting these differences (using differential cryptanalysis), the
changes cancel each other out as they propagate through the rounds of the algorithm, resulting in the exact same final hash
value.

### The Significance and Impact

- **The Birthday Paradox:** Statistically, finding a collision for a 128-bit hash by pure random brute-force (the "birthday
  attack") would require roughly $2^{64}$ operations—computationally heavy, but feasible with modern hardware.
- **Practical Collisions:** In 2004, cryptographer Xiaoyun Wang and her team published a breakthrough method that reduced the
  complexity of finding an MD5 collision to just a few seconds on a standard computer.
- **Real-World Exploits:** Because attackers can generate two different files (such as a benign program and a malicious
  program) that share the exact same MD5 signature, MD5 became completely unsafe for digital signatures and certificates. A
  famous real-world example occurred in 2012 when the **Flame malware** used an MD5 collision attack to forge a fraudulent
  Microsoft code-signing certificate, tricking operating systems into trusting malicious software.
