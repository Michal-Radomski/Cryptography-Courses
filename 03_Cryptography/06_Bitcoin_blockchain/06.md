Bitcoin miners are essentially looking for a specific digital code (called a **nonce**) that allows them to solve a complex
cryptographic puzzle and validate a block of transactions.

When you break down what they are actually searching for during this process, they focus on a few key elements:

- **A Valid Hash:** Miners use powerful computers to run trillions of guesses per second through a cryptographic algorithm
  (SHA-256). They are searching for an output string of numbers and letters—called a **hash**—that falls below a specific
  target number set by the Bitcoin network.
- **The Block Reward:** Finding this winning hash allows a miner to "seal" the next block of transactions and add it to the
  public ledger (the blockchain). As payment for securing the network and doing this heavy computational work, they look to
  collect the **block reward** (newly minted Bitcoin) plus the **transaction fees** attached to all the transfers included in
  that block.
- **Low Electricity Costs & Efficient Hardware:** Because guessing hashes takes an enormous amount of electricity, miners
  constantly look for the cheapest possible power sources and the most efficient specialized computers (called **ASICs**) to
  ensure their operational costs stay lower than the value of the Bitcoin they earn.

A nonce (short for **"number used once"**) is a 32-bit integer—essentially a whole number between 0 and 4,294,967,295—that
miners change constantly to alter a block's data.

**What it looks like**

- **As a decimal number:** Any whole integer within its range, such as `284759102`.
- **As hexadecimal code:** Often displayed by mining software as an 8-character hexadecimal string, such as `4a5c12ef`.

**What it is for**

- **Creating a variable:** Because the transactions inside a block and the timestamp are fixed, a miner needs a variable
  piece of data they can alter freely. The nonce serves this exact purpose.
- **Triggering a new hash:** The cryptographic hash function (SHA-256) is completely unpredictable. Changing the nonce by
  even a single digit (e.g., moving from `1,000` to `1,001`) completely scrambles the resulting output hash.
- **Solving the puzzle:** Miners use the nonce to brute-force combinations, testing millions or trillions of different
  numbers until they generate a hash that meets the network's difficulty target.
