A 51% attack is a scenario where a single miner or organization gains control of more than 50% of a cryptocurrency network's
computing power (hash rate, or processing speed), giving them temporary control over the blockchain's ledger.

With this majority control, the attacker gains specific capabilities over the network's recent history:

- **Double Spending:** This is the primary risk. An attacker can send cryptocurrency to a merchant or exchange to buy
  something, and once they receive the goods, they can use their dominant computing power to secretly mine a private,
  alternative version of the blockchain where that transaction never happened. Because their private chain becomes longer
  than the public chain, the network accepts it as the truth, effectively refunding the coins to the attacker so they can
  spend them again.
- **Blocking Transactions:** They can prevent new transactions from confirming for certain users.
- **Halting Mining:** They can stop other miners from successfully adding new blocks.

**Why it is rare on major networks:** Executing a 51% attack on a massive, highly decentralized network like Bitcoin is
practically and economically nearly impossible. It would require billions of dollars worth of specialized hardware (ASICs)
and immense amounts of electricity—far more than the financial reward the attacker could hope to steal. Such an attack is
much more feasible on smaller, newer, or less popular cryptocurrencies that have lower total hash rates.
