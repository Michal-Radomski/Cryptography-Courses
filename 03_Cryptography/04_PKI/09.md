Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) is the backbone of secure internet communication, managing the digital certificates and
public-private key pairs that keep data encrypted and trusted. Because PKI controls trust across systems, it is a high-value
target for adversaries.

The primary categories of attacks and vulnerabilities targeting PKI include:

### 1. Certificate Authority (CA) Compromise

Certificate Authorities are the trusted entities that issue digital certificates. If an attacker breaches a CA's
infrastructure or steals its root/intermediate private keys, they can issue valid, trusted certificates for any domain on the
internet.

- **Impact:** Attackers can craft fraudulent certificates to impersonate legitimate websites (like a bank or email provider),
  launch undetectable Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) attacks, and intercept encrypted traffic on a massive scale.

### 2. Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) and Rogue Certificates

Adversaries attempt to position themselves between a user and a server. If they manage to install a rogue or fraudulently
obtained certificate on a compromised network device, they can trick users into trusting a fake connection.

- **Impact:** The attacker can read, modify, or steal sensitive data—such as login credentials or financial information—while
  both the user and the server believe they have a secure, direct connection.

### 3. Private Key Theft and Exposure

The security of PKI relies entirely on the secrecy of private keys. If developers or administrators accidentally leak private
keys (such as committing them to public code repositories) or if malware steals them from a server's storage, the security
model breaks down.

- **Impact:** Anyone who gets hold of a private key can decrypt past or present traffic destined for that server and
  impersonate the rightful owner.

### 4. Cryptographic Weaknesses and Algorithm Degradation

Over time, computational power increases and mathematical flaws are discovered in older encryption algorithms (such as weak
hashing functions like MD5 or SHA-1, or short RSA key lengths).

- **Impact:** Attackers can use techniques like collision attacks or brute-forcing to forge signatures or crack keys,
  allowing them to spoof certificates.

### 5. Revocation and Validation Exploits

When a certificate is compromised, it needs to be revoked. However, attackers can target the mechanisms used to check
revocation status—such as Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP) or Certificate Revocation Lists (CRLs).

- **Impact:** By blocking revocation checks or forcing "fail-open" behaviors on client systems, attackers can trick browsers
  into accepting certificates that should have been rejected.

### 6. Operational Mismanagement and Expired Assets

Many PKI vulnerabilities do not stem from sophisticated hacking, but from human error or poor lifecycle management.

- **Impact:** When certificates expire without renewal, services go down, forcing organizations into emergency states where
  proper security protocols might be bypassed. Conversely, lax internal controls can lead to shadow IT issuing unauthorized
  certificates that lack proper oversight.
