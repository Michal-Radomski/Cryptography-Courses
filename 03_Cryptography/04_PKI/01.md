## What is PKI (Public Key Infrastructure)?

**Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)** is a comprehensive framework of encryption, software, hardware, policies, and procedures
needed to create, manage, distribute, use, store, and revoke digital certificates.

In simple terms, PKI is the security system that makes the internet safe (such as enabling HTTPS on websites). It ensures
that the digital identities of servers, devices, and users can be trusted by binding a public key to a specific entity using
a **Digital Certificate**.

Key components of PKI include:

- **Certificate Authority (CA):** A trusted third-party organization (like Let's Encrypt, DigiCert, or Sectigo) that issues
  digital certificates.
- **Digital Certificate:** An electronic document that proves the ownership of a public key. It includes the website name,
  the owner's details, the CA's digital signature, and an expiration date.

## How PKI Defeats a Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) Attack

A **Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) attack** happens when a hacker secretly intercepts and relays communications between two parties
who believe they are directly communicating with each other (for example, you and your online bank).

Here is how PKI stops these attacks dead in your tracks:

### 1. The Trap Without PKI

If a website only used basic encryption without PKI, a hacker could position themselves between your browser and the website.
They could intercept your traffic and present _their own_ fake encryption key. Your browser wouldn't know the difference, and
the hacker could read or change all your data.

### 2. The Verification with PKI

When you visit a secure website (using HTTPS), the server must present its **Digital Certificate** to your browser.

- **Trusted Authority:** Your browser checks who signed the website's certificate. Because your operating system or browser
  comes pre-loaded with a list of trusted **Certificate Authorities (CAs)**, it can verify if the certificate is legitimate.
- **Identity Binding:** The certificate proves that the website you are talking to actually owns that domain name. An
  attacker cannot easily forge a fake certificate because they cannot trick a trusted CA into signing a certificate for a
  domain they don't own.
- **The Result:** If the certificate is invalid, untrusted, or doesn't match the website address, your browser immediately
  displays a prominent security warning (like _"Your connection is not private"_) and blocks the connection, stopping the
  MitM attack before it can steal your data.
