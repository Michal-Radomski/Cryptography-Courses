Here are the technical details regarding the operating system, frequency, and voltage of smart card chips:

### 1. Operating System on the Chip

EMV smart cards do not run standard operating systems like Windows or Linux. Instead, they run specialized, highly secure,
lightweight operating systems designed to execute small applications (called applets) within a protected environment. Common
examples include:

- **Java Card OS:** The most popular platform, allowing secure Java applets to run on the card.
- **MULTOS:** A highly secure operating system designed specifically for smart cards with strict multi-application security.
- **Proprietary OS:** Some card manufacturers develop custom, closed-source operating systems optimized for their specific
  hardware.

### 2. Frequency of Communication (Contactless)

For contactless (NFC) cards and mobile payment devices, the communication frequency is universally standardized at:

- **13.56 MHz:** This is the standard high-frequency (HF) RFID/NFC band used globally for payment systems, transit cards, and
  access control.

### 3. Voltage Generated on the Card

The voltage requirements depend on whether the card is being used via contact or contactless methods:

- **Contact Cards:** When inserted into a terminal, the terminal supplies power through physical contacts. Modern chips
  typically operate at low voltages such as **1.8V, 3V, or 5V** to minimize power consumption and protect the secure
  hardware.
- **Contactless Cards:** When tapped, the card has no internal battery. The electromagnetic field from the terminal induces a
  tiny alternating current (AC) in the card's internal antenna, which is then rectified and regulated internally by the chip
  to a low operating DC voltage (typically around **1.8V to 3.3V**) to power its processor for the duration of the
  transaction.
