The Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange is a cryptographic protocol that allows two parties to establish a shared secret over an
insecure communication channel without transmitting the secret itself. This shared secret can then be used to encrypt
subsequent communications using symmetric-key cryptography.

### The Analogy (Color Mixing)

To understand the mathematics, it helps to use the classic paint-mixing analogy:

1. **Public Agreement:** Alice and Bob publicly agree on a starting color of paint, say yellow.
2. **Secret Colors:** Alice picks a secret color (e.g., secret red) and Bob picks a secret color (e.g., secret blue).
3. **Mixing:** Alice mixes her secret red with the public yellow to make orange. Bob mixes his secret blue with the public
   yellow to make light green.
4. **Exchange:** They exchange their mixed colors (orange and light green) over the public channel. Because the colors are
   mixed, an eavesdropper cannot easily separate them to figure out Alice's or Bob's original secret colors.
5. **Final Secret:** Alice takes the light green Bob sent and adds her secret red. Bob takes the orange Alice sent and adds
   his secret blue. Both end up with the exact same final secret color (a brownish mixture), while the individual secret
   colors remain hidden.

### The Mathematical Steps

In practice, the algorithm relies on modular arithmetic and the difficulty of solving the discrete logarithm problem.

1. **Setup:**

- A large prime number $p$ and a base $g$ (a primitive root modulo $p$) are chosen and shared publicly.

2. **Key Generation:**

- **Alice** chooses a private integer $a$ and computes her public value:

$$A = g^a \pmod p$$

- **Bob** chooses a private integer $b$ and computes his public value:

$$B = g^b \pmod p$$

3. **Exchange:**

- Alice sends $A$ to Bob, and Bob sends $B$ to Alice.

4. **Secret Computation:**

- **Alice** takes Bob's public value $B$ and raises it to her private key $a$:

$$s = B^a \pmod p$$

- **Bob** takes Alice's public value $A$ and raises it to his private key $b$:

$$s = A^b \pmod p$$

Because of the mathematical properties of exponents ($(g^b)^a = g^{ba} = g^{ab} = (g^a)^b$), both Alice and Bob compute the
exact same shared secret $s$. An observer who only sees $p$, $g$, $A$, and $B$ cannot easily compute $s$ because doing so
requires solving the discrete logarithm problem, which is computationally infeasible for large numbers.
