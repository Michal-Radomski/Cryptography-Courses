## How EMV Chip Cards Work

The chip on a credit or debit card is actually a tiny, highly secure microcomputer (often called a **smart card chip**).
Unlike a magnetic stripe that just holds static data, this chip contains a CPU, secure memory, and cryptographic software.

When you make a transaction, the chip doesn't just hand over your card number; it actively communicates with the payment
terminal to run a mini-program that generates a unique, one-time digital signature for that specific purchase.

---

## How It Gets Energy to Work

The chip has no internal battery of its own. Instead, it relies entirely on power supplied by the payment terminal. How it
receives that power depends on how you use the card:

- **For Inserted Cards (Contact):** When you push your card into the terminal, the visible gold metal pads on the chip
  physically touch metal pins inside the reader. The terminal immediately sends a small electric current through these
  contacts to power up the chip's processor.
- **For Tapped Cards (Contactless / NFC):** When you tap your card or phone, it uses **electromagnetic induction**. The
  payment terminal emits a radio frequency field. Inside the card, a tiny coiled copper wire (an antenna) catches this radio
  wave, and the energy from the wave converts into a tiny electrical current that wakes up the chip instantly.

Once the chip receives this burst of power, it boots up in milliseconds, runs the security checks, approves or declines the
cryptographic challenge, and shuts down as soon as you remove the card or finish the tap.

## The Cryptographic Handshake

When you insert or tap your card, a multi-step verification process happens in milliseconds before the transaction is
approved:

### 1. Introduction and Capabilities

- **The Terminal Speaks:** When the chip gets power, it tells the terminal what features and cryptographic algorithms it
  supports.
- **Selection:** The terminal picks the best matching payment application (e.g., Visa, Mastercard) available on the card.

### 2. Card Authentication (Checking if the card is real)

- **Public Key Check:** The terminal uses a pre-installed root certificate from the bank to verify the digital certificate
  stored on the card's chip. This proves the card was actually issued by a legitimate bank and isn't a fake counterfeit.

### 3. Cardholder Verification (Checking if you are the owner)

- **Proving It's You:** Depending on the setup, the terminal verifies your identity. This could mean checking your PIN
  against the chip, prompting for a signature, or using your phone's biometric lock (Face ID/fingerprint) for contactless
  mobile payments.

### 4. Cryptogram Generation (Creating the unique signature)

- **The Challenge:** The terminal sends a unique set of transaction data to the card—including the amount, currency, date,
  and a random number (nonce).
- **The Signature:** The chip uses its internal **private key** to sign this data, creating a unique cryptographic code
  called an **Application Cryptogram**.
- **Sending Back:** The card sends this cryptogram back to the terminal. Because the amount and random number are baked into
  the signature, this specific code can never be reused for another purchase.

### 5. Bank Approval

- **Final Check:** The terminal sends the cryptogram and transaction details to the bank over the internet. The bank uses the
  card's corresponding **public key** to verify the cryptogram. If it matches, the bank approves the payment.
