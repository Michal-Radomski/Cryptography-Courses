The modulo operation finds the remainder after the division of one number by another. Written mathematically as $a \pmod n$,
it calculates the left-over value when $a$ (the dividend) is divided by $n$ (the divisor or modulus).

### Examples

- $10 \pmod 3 = 1$ (since 10 divided by 3 is 3, with a remainder of 1)
- $17 \pmod 5 = 2$ (since 17 divided by 5 is 3, with a remainder of 2)
- $14 \pmod 7 = 0$ (since 14 divides evenly by 7, leaving no remainder)

### Common Applications

- **Clock Arithmetic:** Time systems wrap around after reaching a certain value. A 12-hour clock operates modulo 12. For
  example, if it is currently 10:00 and you add 5 hours, the time is $(10 + 5) \pmod{12} = 3$ o'clock.
- **Computer Science:** Programmers use modulo (often represented as `%` in code) to check if a number is even or odd
  (`x % 2 == 0`), cycle through arrays or lists in a loop, or implement hash tables.
- **Cryptography:** As seen in algorithms like Diffie-Hellman and RSA, modular arithmetic acts as a one-way function. It is
  easy to compute large exponents modulo a prime number, but extremely difficult to reverse the process without knowing the
  private keys.
