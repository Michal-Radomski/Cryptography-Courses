## What is a Digital Signature?

A **digital signature** is a mathematical technique used to validate the authenticity and integrity of a digital message,
software, or document. Think of it as the digital equivalent of a traditional handwritten signature or a physical seal, but
with far higher security. It proves two crucial things: that the message genuinely originated from the claimed sender, and
that it was not altered in transit.

---

## How It Works

Digital signatures rely on **public-key cryptography** (asymmetric cryptography), which uses a pair of mathematically linked
keys:

- **Private Key:** Kept strictly secret by the owner and used to _create_ the signature.
- **Public Key:** Distributed openly to anyone and used to _verify_ the signature.

When a document is signed, a cryptographic hash of the document is encrypted with the sender's private key. The recipient
then uses the public key to verify the signature. If the keys and hashes match, the signature is authentic.

---

## Common Algorithms Used

Several cryptographic algorithms power digital signatures today, balancing computational efficiency and robust security:

- **RSA (Rivest–Shamir–Adleman):** One of the oldest and most widely used algorithms. It relies on the mathematical
  difficulty of factoring the product of two very large prime numbers.
- **DSA (Digital Signature Algorithm):** A federal standard established by NIST specifically for digital signatures, based on
  the mathematics of discrete logarithms.
- **ECDSA (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm):** A variant of DSA that uses elliptic curve cryptography. It provides
  the same security as RSA while using much smaller key sizes, making it ideal for smartphones and cryptocurrencies like
  Bitcoin.
- **EdDSA (Edwards-curve Digital Signature Algorithm):** A modern, high-performance signature scheme that offers fast signing
  and verification speeds while protecting against certain side-channel attacks.
