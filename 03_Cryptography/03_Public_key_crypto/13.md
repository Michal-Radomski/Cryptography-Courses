Public-key cryptography plays a crucial role in securing modern credit card transactions, both in physical stores and online.
Instead of relying on static information that can easily be copied, it uses mathematical key pairs to prove that a card or
transaction is authentic.

Here is how public-key cryptography is applied in credit card authentication:

### 1. EMV Chip Cards (In-Store Purchases)

When you insert or tap a smart card (chip card) at a terminal, a process called **Dynamic Data Authentication (DDA)** or
**Combined Data Authentication (CDA)** takes place:

- **The Chip's Secret:** Inside the microchip embedded in the credit card is a secure cryptoprocessor holding a private key
  unique to that card.
- **The Challenge:** The payment terminal sends a random number (a challenge) to the card.
- **The Signature:** The card uses its internal **private key** to sign that random number along with transaction details
  (like the amount and date), creating a unique digital signature called an **application cryptogram**.
- **The Verification:** The bank (issuer) or the terminal uses the card's **public key** to verify this cryptogram. Because a
  new cryptogram is generated for every single transaction, copying data from the terminal is useless to fraudsters—stolen
  data cannot be used to create a counterfeit card.

### 2. 3D Secure (Online Purchases)

For online shopping (card-not-present transactions), a protocol called **3D Secure (3DS)** is used to authenticate the
cardholder:

- **Digital Certificates:** Banks and payment networks (like Visa or Mastercard) use public-key infrastructure (PKI) digital
  certificates to establish secure, trusted communication channels between the merchant, the payment gateway, and the
  card-issuing bank.
- **Device & Biometric Binding:** Modern 3DS (such as 3DS 2.x) frequently integrates with device-level cryptography. For
  example, when you use a smartphone fingerprint, face scan, or passkey to approve an online purchase, your device uses an
  internal secure enclave housing a private key to cryptographically sign the transaction approval. The issuing bank verifies
  this using your stored public key.

### Why It Matters

By utilizing public-key cryptography, the payment system ensures that:

- **Card data cannot be cloned** from a point-of-sale terminal reader.
- **Private keys never leave** the secure chip on your card or device, meaning hackers cannot steal them during a data breach
  at a merchant's database.
