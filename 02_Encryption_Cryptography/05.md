Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) refers to cryptographic algorithms designed to be secure against both classical and quantum
computers, protecting digital infrastructure from future quantum-enabled decryption threats.

### Why PQC is Needed

Traditional public-key cryptography—such as RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)—relies on mathematical
problems like integer factorization and the discrete logarithm problem. While these operations are computationally impossible
for classical computers to reverse in a reasonable timeframe, Shor's algorithm running on a sufficiently powerful quantum
computer can solve them efficiently. This introduces a major risk known as "harvest now, decrypt later," where sensitive data
intercepted over networks today can be stored and retroactively decrypted once quantum hardware matures.

### Core Mathematical Approaches

Post-quantum cryptography relies on alternative mathematical structures that lack the predictable algebraic shortcuts
exploited by quantum algorithms:

- **Lattice-Based Cryptography:** Based on the hardness of finding shortest vectors in high-dimensional geometric grids. It
  forms the backbone of most modern PQC standards due to its strong security and performance balance.
- **Hash-Based Cryptography:** Relies exclusively on the security properties of cryptographic hash functions, making it
  exceptionally resilient against mathematical breakthroughs.
- **Code-Based Cryptography:** Built upon the difficulty of decoding random linear error-correcting codes, offering a
  time-tested alternative to lattice math.
- **Multivariate Cryptography:** Based on the complexity of solving systems of multivariate polynomial equations over finite
  fields.

### NIST Standards and Implementation

The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) finalized a portfolio of official post-quantum standards to
secure government and commercial systems:

- **FIPS 203 (ML-KEM):** Module Lattice-Based Key Encapsulation Mechanism, used for secure key exchange and replacing
  traditional Diffie-Hellman methods.
- **FIPS 204 (ML-DSA):** Module Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm, used for authenticating digital signatures, code
  signing, and certificates.
- **FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA):** Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm, providing a conservative backup signature
  standard based entirely on hash security.

To bridge the gap safely, modern web browsers, operating systems, and security protocols increasingly implement these
algorithms in **hybrid modes**—combining classical encryption with post-quantum primitives to ensure immediate resilience
without sacrificing backward compatibility.
