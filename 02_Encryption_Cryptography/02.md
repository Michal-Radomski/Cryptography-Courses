Shamir's Secret Sharing is a cryptographic algorithm that divides a secret into multiple unique parts—called
shares—distributed among a group of participants, such that a specific minimum number of shares are required to reconstruct
the original secret.

Invented by Adi Shamir (co-creator of RSA) in 1979, the scheme is based on polynomial interpolation and offers
**information-theoretic security**, meaning that possessing fewer than the required number of shares provides mathematically
zero information about the secret, even to an attacker with infinite computing power.

### How It Works (The Threshold Scheme)

To split a secret $S$ among $n$ participants such that any group of $k$ participants (where $k \le n$) can unlock it—a
$(k, n)$ threshold scheme—the algorithm follows these steps:

1. **Polynomial Generation:** A random polynomial of degree $k-1$ is constructed:

$$f(x) = a_0 + a_1x + a_2x^2 + \dots + a_{k-1}x^{k-1} \pmod p$$

- The constant term $a_0$ is set to the secret $S$.
- The remaining coefficients ($a_1, a_2, \dots, a_{k-1}$) are chosen as random numbers.
- All calculations are performed modulo a large prime number $p$ that is larger than both the secret and the number of
  participants.

2. **Share Distribution:** The system evaluates the polynomial at $n$ distinct points (e.g., $x = 1, 2, \dots, n$) to
   generate $n$ coordinate pairs. Each participant receives one share, which is a point $(x, f(x))$ on the polynomial curve.
3. **Secret Reconstruction:**

- If fewer than $k$ participants (e.g., $k-1$ shares) attempt to find the secret, it is mathematically impossible because a
  unique polynomial cannot be determined from too few points.
- When $k$ or more participants bring their shares together, they can use **Lagrange interpolation** to reconstruct the
  polynomial $f(x)$ and solve for $f(0)$, instantly recovering the secret $S$.

### Key Applications

- **Cryptographic Key Management:** Splitting master encryption keys or cryptocurrency wallet seed phrases across multiple
  trusted individuals or secure geographic locations to prevent a single point of failure or compromise.
- **Multi-Party Authorization:** Requiring a quorum of administrators (e.g., any 3 out of 5 board members) to authorize
  high-security actions, such as launching sensitive systems or accessing secure vaults.
