## Overview of Random Number Generators (RNG)

A **Random Number Generator (RNG)** is a system—either hardware-based or software-based—designed to generate a sequence of
numbers or symbols that cannot be reasonably predicted better than by random chance. RNGs are essential for everything from
video games and statistics to high-security cryptography.

---

### The Two Main Types of RNGs

Computers are deterministic by nature, meaning they follow strict instructions and usually don't do things "by chance."
Because of this, RNGs are split into two major categories:

#### 1. Pseudo-Random Number Generators (PRNG)

- **How it works:** PRNGs use mathematical formulas (algorithms) and an initial starting value called a **seed** to generate
  a sequence of numbers.
- **The "Pseudo" part:** Because it relies on math and a seed, the sequence isn't truly random—if you know the algorithm and
  the exact seed, you can predict every number that comes next.
- **Use Cases:** Video games (like random item drops or map generation), simulations, and statistical sampling where speed is
  important and absolute security isn't required.

#### 2. True Random Number Generators (TRNG)

- **How it works:** Instead of a math formula, TRNGs measure unpredictable, chaotic **physical phenomena** in the real world.
  Examples include atmospheric noise (static), thermal noise from computer hardware, or radioactive decay.
- **The result:** Because physical noise is completely chaotic, the numbers generated are truly random and impossible to
  predict or reproduce.
- **Use Cases:** High-security cryptography, generating secure encryption keys, and secure online gambling.

---

### Why RNGs Matter in Cybersecurity

In cryptography, the unpredictability of an RNG is a matter of life and death for data security. If a hacker can figure out
or guess the PRNG algorithm and seed used to generate an encryption key, they can break the encryption. This is why modern
operating systems use **CSPRNGs (Cryptographically Secure Pseudo-Random Number Generators)**, which combine mathematical
algorithms with unpredictable real-world inputs (like mouse movements or system timings) to create safe, unpredictable keys.
