## Overview of the Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA)

The **Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA)** is a federal standard for digital signatures, established by the National Institute
of Standards and Technology (NIST) in 1991. Like RSA and ECC, DSA is an asymmetric cryptographic algorithm used to verify the
authenticity and integrity of digital messages or documents.

---

### How DSA Works

DSA relies on a mathematical concept known as the **discrete logarithm problem** over finite fields rather than prime
factorization (like RSA).

- **Key Pair Generation:** A user generates a private key (used for signing) and a public key (used for verification) based
  on specific mathematical parameters (large prime numbers).
- **Signing:** The sender uses their **private key** along with a cryptographic hash of the document to generate a signature
  (consisting of two large integers, usually denoted as $r$ and $s$).
- **Verification:** Anyone receiving the document uses the sender's **public key** to mathematically verify that the
  signature matches the document's hash. If they match, the signature is authentic and unaltered.

---

### DSA vs. Modern Alternatives

While DSA laid vital groundwork for digital signatures, it has largely been superseded in modern systems by newer variants:

- **ECDSA (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm):** The elliptic-curve counterpart to DSA. Just like ECC compared to
  RSA, ECDSA provides the same level of security as traditional DSA but with much smaller key sizes and faster performance.
- **Ed25519:** A newer, highly secure, and fast signature scheme based on Twisted Edwards curves, commonly used in modern web
  protocols and cryptocurrencies.
