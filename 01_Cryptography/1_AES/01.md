## What is AES?

**AES** stands for **Advanced Encryption Standard**. It is one of the most secure and widely used encryption algorithms in
the world today. It is a symmetric block cipher, meaning it uses the exact same secret key to both encrypt data (turn it into
unreadable ciphertext) and decrypt it (turn it back into readable plaintext).

---

## How Does AES Work?

AES operates on fixed blocks of data—specifically, blocks of **128 bits** at a time. Depending on how high security needs to
be, it can use three different key lengths:

- **AES-128:** Uses a 128-bit key.
- **AES-192:** Uses a 192-bit key.
- **AES-256:** Uses a 256-bit key (the strongest option, often used for top-secret government data).

During encryption, the data goes through multiple "rounds" of complex mathematical transformations—including substituting
bytes, shifting rows, mixing columns, and combining the data with the secret key.

Because AES-256 uses 14 rounds of these operations, trying to crack it by guessing every possible key combination (brute
force) would take modern supercomputers billions of years.

---

## Where Do You Use AES Every Day?

You rely on AES constantly to keep your personal information private online:

- **Wi-Fi Security:** Your home router uses AES (via WPA2 or WPA3 protocols) to encrypt the data sent over the air between
  your devices and the internet.
- **Secure Messaging:** Apps that feature end-to-end encryption often rely on AES to lock down your messages.
- **Device Encryption:** Features like FileVault on Mac or BitLocker on Windows use AES to protect all the files stored on
  your hard drive if your laptop is ever lost or stolen.
