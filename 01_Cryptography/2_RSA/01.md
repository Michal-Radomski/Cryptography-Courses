## Overview of the RSA Algorithm

The **RSA** algorithm (named after its inventors Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman) is one of the first and most widely used
**asymmetric cryptographic algorithms**. Unlike symmetric ciphers like AES—which use the exact same secret key for both
encryption and decryption—RSA uses a **key pair**: a public key and a private key.

---

### How Asymmetric Encryption Works

- **The Public Key:** Can be shared openly with anyone. When someone wants to send you a secure message, they use your public
  key to encrypt it.
- **The Private Key:** Must be kept strictly secret by you. Once data is encrypted with your public key, **only** your
  private key can unlock and decrypt it.

---

### The Core Mathematical Principle

RSA's security relies on a practical one-way mathematical function: **prime factorization**.

- It is very easy and fast for computers to multiply two extremely large prime numbers together to create a massive composite
  number.
- However, if you are only given that massive composite number, it is computationally infeasible (taking standard computers
  millions of years) to figure out what the original two prime numbers were. That massive composite number forms the basis of
  the public key, while the hidden prime factors are used to create the private key.

---

### RSA vs. AES: When to Use Which?

While both protect data, they serve different purposes in modern security:

- **RSA (Asymmetric):** Excellent for securely exchanging keys over an insecure network or creating digital signatures, but
  it is computationally slow and inefficient for encrypting large amounts of data.
- **AES (Symmetric):** Incredibly fast and efficient at encrypting large volumes of data, but requires a secure way to share
  the secret key beforehand.

Because of this, modern security protocols (like HTTPS/TLS) combine both: they use **RSA** to safely negotiate and exchange a
temporary secret key, and then switch to **AES** to encrypt the actual web traffic.
